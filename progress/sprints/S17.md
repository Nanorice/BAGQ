#### Calculus — 3 questions

**Q1 (numerical).** A quantity decays as `dN/dt = −0.2N` with `N(0) = 500`. Find `N(t)`, and find
the half-life.

**Q2 (conceptual).** You are maximising `f(x,y)` subject to `g(x,y) = c`. Explain in one sentence
why the gradients of `f` and `g` must be parallel at the optimum.

**Q3 (twist).** Find the maximum of `f(x,y) = xy` subject to `x + y = 10` two ways: by
substitution, and by Lagrange multipliers. Then say what `λ` equals and what it means here.

<details>
<summary>Answers — calculus</summary>

**Q1.** Separable: `dN/N = −0.2 dt` → `ln N = −0.2t + C` → `N(t) = 500e^{−0.2t}`.
Half-life: `500e^{−0.2t} = 250` → `e^{−0.2t} = 1/2` → `t = ln2/0.2 ≈ **3.47**`.

*Note the half-life does not depend on `N(0)` — that is the same structural fact as
memorylessness in the exponential distribution, which is not a coincidence: both are
`dY/dt ∝ Y`.*

**Q2.** At a constrained optimum you cannot improve `f` by moving along the constraint surface.
Any component of `∇f` tangent to that surface would let you improve, so `∇f` must be entirely
perpendicular to it — and `∇g` is also perpendicular to it by definition. Two vectors
perpendicular to the same surface are parallel: `∇f = λ∇g`.

**Q3.** *Substitution:* `y = 10 − x`, so `f = x(10−x) = 10x − x²`. `f' = 10 − 2x = 0` → `x = 5`,
`y = 5`, `f = **25**`.

*Lagrange:* `∇f = (y, x)`, `∇g = (1,1)`. So `y = λ` and `x = λ` → `x = y`, and with `x+y=10`,
`x = y = 5`, `λ = **5**`.

**What `λ` means:** raising the budget from 10 to 11 raises the maximum product by ≈5 (exactly:
30.25 − 25 = 5.25, and `λ` is the first-order rate). That is the shadow price.

</details>

#### Linear algebra — 3 questions

**Q1 (numerical).** For `A = [[4,0],[3,-2]]`, find the eigenvalues. Is `A` diagonalisable? Is it
symmetric, and does that matter for your answer?

**Q2 (conceptual).** A covariance matrix built from 3 assets over 2 days of returns comes out
singular. Explain why that had to happen, in terms of rank.

**Q3 (twist).** You are given a correlation matrix `[[1, 0.9, −0.9], [0.9, 1, −0.9],
[−0.9, −0.9, 1]]`. Without computing all three eigenvalues, argue whether this can be a valid
correlation matrix. *(Hint: consider `xᵀCx` for a well-chosen `x`.)*

<details>
<summary>Answers — linear algebra</summary>

**Q1.** Lower triangular, so the eigenvalues are the diagonal: **λ = 4, −2**. Two distinct
eigenvalues in a 2×2 means two independent eigenvectors, so **yes, diagonalisable**.

`A` is **not** symmetric. Symmetry would have *guaranteed* real eigenvalues and orthogonal
eigenvectors, but it is not necessary for diagonalisability — distinct eigenvalues suffice. The
eigenvectors here are not orthogonal.

**Q2.** The sample covariance of `n` assets from `T` observations has rank at most `T−1` (the
`−1` from subtracting the mean). With `T = 2` days, rank ≤ 1, but the matrix is 3×3 — so at least
two eigenvalues are exactly zero and it is singular.

*This is the `N > T` problem, and it is why a naive Markowitz optimiser blows up on a realistic
universe: the inverse does not exist.*

**Q3.** Take `x = (1, 1, 1)`. Then `xᵀCx` sums every entry:
`3 (diagonal) + 2(0.9) + 2(−0.9) + 2(−0.9) = 3 + 1.8 − 1.8 − 1.8 = **1.2**` — positive, so this
particular `x` does not disprove it.

Try `x = (1, 1, −1)`: `3 + 2(0.9) − 2(−0.9) − 2(−0.9) = 3 + 1.8 + 1.8 + 1.8 = 8.4`, also positive.

**The real argument:** if A and B are both 0.9-correlated with each other, and both −0.9 with C,
that is self-consistent — A and B move together, C moves opposite to both. The structure is
plausible. Computing it out gives eigenvalues ≈ 2.8, 0.1, 0.1, all ≥ 0, so **it is valid**.

*The transferable point: a single `x` giving a positive value proves nothing; PSD requires it for
**all** `x`. To disprove you need one witness, to prove you need the eigenvalues.*

</details>

#### Discrete distributions — 5 questions

**Q1 (conceptual).** Both the binomial and the Poisson can describe "number of events". State the
condition under which the Poisson is the right choice, and name the quantity that must be
constant for it to hold.

**Q2 (conceptual).** For a Poisson, `E[X] = Var(X) = λ`. You fit a Poisson to real order-arrival
data and find the sample variance is three times the sample mean. What does that tell you, and
what would you do about it?

**Q3 (numerical).** A trader wins each bet independently with probability `0.4`. In 10 bets, find
`P(exactly 3 wins)` and `E[wins]`.

**Q4 (numerical).** Emails arrive at 3 per hour, Poisson. Find `P(no emails in a 20-minute
window)`.

**Q5 (twist).** You roll a fair die repeatedly. What is the expected number of rolls until you
have seen **all six faces** at least once?

<details>
<summary>Answers — discrete distributions</summary>

**Q1.** Poisson applies as a limit of the binomial when trials are many, each success is rare,
and `np` stays finite — formally `n → ∞`, `p → 0`, with **`λ = np` held constant**. Practically:
use it when you know the *rate* but there is no natural fixed number of trials.

**Q2.** Variance three times the mean is **overdispersion**, and it means the Poisson assumption
is wrong — specifically the assumption that arrivals are independent with constant rate. Real
order flow **clusters**: one large order raises the near-term probability of another.

*What to do:* move to a model that allows the rate to vary — negative binomial (a Poisson with a
gamma-distributed rate), or a Hawkes process if the clustering is self-exciting. **Consequence if
ignored:** a Poisson understates the tail, so any risk number built on it understates the
probability of a burst.

**Q3.** Binomial(10, 0.4). `P(X=3) = C(10,3)(0.4)³(0.6)⁷ = 120 · 0.064 · 0.0279936 ≈ **0.215**`.
`E[X] = np = **4**`.

**Q4.** 20 minutes = 1/3 hour, so `λ' = 3 × 1/3 = 1`. `P(X=0) = e^{−1} ≈ **0.368**`.

**Q5.** Coupon collector. Once you hold `k` distinct faces, the probability a roll gives a new one
is `(6−k)/6`, so the wait is geometric with mean `6/(6−k)`. Sum over `k = 0…5`:

`E = 6/6 + 6/5 + 6/4 + 6/3 + 6/2 + 6/1 = 6(1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + 1/5 + 1/6) = 6 · 2.45 = **14.7**`

*Linearity of expectation across stages that are **not** independent — the waits overlap in time,
and linearity does not care. That is the whole trick.*

</details>

#### Continuous distributions — 5 questions

**Q1 (conceptual).** Explain why the exponential is the only continuous distribution that is
memoryless, in terms of what memorylessness forces its survival function to satisfy.

**Q2 (conceptual).** A colleague computes a 99% one-day VaR using `1.96` standard deviations.
What have they got wrong, and by how much?

**Q3 (numerical).** `X ~ Uniform(2, 10)`. Find `E[X]`, `Var(X)`, and `P(X > 7 | X > 5)`.

**Q4 (numerical).** Daily returns are `N(0, 0.02²)`. Find the probability of a daily loss worse
than 4%, using the 68/95/99.7 anchors.

**Q5 (twist).** `X ~ Exp(λ)`. Let `Y = ⌊X⌋`, the integer part. What is the distribution of `Y`,
and why should that not be surprising?

<details>
<summary>Answers — continuous distributions</summary>

**Q1.** Memorylessness says `S(s+t) = S(s)S(t)` for the survival function `S(x) = P(X>x)`. That
is Cauchy's functional equation, and its only solutions that are monotone and satisfy `S(0)=1`
are `S(x) = e^{−λx}` — which is exactly the exponential. **The functional equation has one
family of solutions, so the characterisation is forced, not merely observed.**

**Q2.** `1.96` is the **two-tailed** 95% z-value. VaR is **one-tailed** — you only care about
losses — and the one-tailed 99% value is **2.326**.

They have understated the capital requirement by a factor of `2.326/1.96 ≈ **1.19**`, about 19%.
*Two errors compounded: wrong tail count and wrong confidence level. This is a production bug,
not a quiz slip.*

**Q3.** `E[X] = (2+10)/2 = **6**`. `Var(X) = (10−2)²/12 = 64/12 ≈ **5.33**`.

`P(X>7 | X>5) = P(X>7)/P(X>5) = (3/8)/(5/8) = **0.6**`.

*Note this is **not** `P(X>2)` — the uniform is **not** memoryless. Conditioning genuinely
changes the remaining distribution, which is exactly what distinguishes it from the exponential.*

**Q4.** 4% is `2σ` below the mean. `P(Z < −2) ≈ (1 − 0.95)/2 = **0.025**`, so about **2.5%**, or
roughly one day in 40.

*Real return distributions are fat-tailed, so the true frequency is higher — the normal
assumption is the optimistic one.*

**Q5.** `P(Y = k) = P(k ≤ X < k+1) = e^{−λk} − e^{−λ(k+1)} = e^{−λk}(1 − e^{−λ})`.

Writing `p = 1 − e^{−λ}`, this is `P(Y=k) = (1−p)^k p` — **geometric**, on `{0,1,2,…}`.

**Why unsurprising:** the geometric is the discrete memoryless distribution and the exponential is
the continuous one. Chopping a memoryless continuous clock into integer ticks has to leave you
with the memoryless discrete one — there is nowhere else it could land.

</details>

#### Combinatorics — 5 questions

Studied last sprint, first test now.

**Q1 (conceptual).** Explain when you divide a multinomial coefficient by `m!` and when you do
not, and give one example of each.

**Q2 (conceptual).** State the stars-and-bars formula for non-negative integer solutions, and say
in one sentence why the bijection is one-to-one in both directions.

**Q3 (numerical).** How many distinct arrangements are there of the letters in `BOOKKEEPER`?

**Q4 (numerical).** How many non-negative integer solutions are there to `x₁+x₂+x₃ = 12` with
`x₁ ≥ 2` and `x₃ ≤ 4`?

**Q5 (twist).** Eight people at a round table. How many seatings are there if two specific people
refuse to sit next to each other? *(Rotations of the same arrangement count as identical.)*

<details>
<summary>Answers — combinatorics</summary>

**Q1.** Divide by `m!` when the `m` groups are **indistinguishable**; do not when they carry
identities.

*Divide:* splitting 12 people into 4 unlabelled teams of 3 — `12!/(3!)⁴ / 4!`, because "Team A =
{1,2,3}, Team B = {4,5,6}" and the swap of those labels are the same partition.
*Do not divide:* dealing bridge hands to North/South/East/West — the seats have identities, so
North holding a hand is a different outcome from South holding it.

**Q2.** Non-negative solutions to `x₁+⋯+x_k = n` number `C(n+k−1, k−1)`.

**Both directions:** every arrangement of `n` stars and `k−1` bars reads off exactly one solution
(the gaps between bars give the `xᵢ`), and every solution writes down as exactly one arrangement.
A correspondence in only one direction would not equate the counts.

**Q3.** `BOOKKEEPER` has 10 letters: B×1, O×2, K×2, E×3, P×1, R×1.
`10!/(1!·2!·2!·3!·1!·1!) = 3,628,800/(2·2·6) = 3,628,800/24 = **151,200**`.

**Q4.** Pre-allocate 2 to `x₁`, leaving `x₁'+x₂+x₃ = 10` with everything non-negative:
`C(10+3−1, 2) = C(12,2) = 66`.

Now subtract the violations `x₃ ≥ 5`: pre-allocate 5 more, leaving a sum of 5:
`C(5+3−1, 2) = C(7,2) = 21`.

`66 − 21 = **45**`.

*Lower bound by substitution, upper bound by subtracting violations — the two halves of the
topic meeting on one problem.*

**Q5.** Total circular seatings of 8: fix one person to kill rotations, `(8−1)! = 5040`.

Seatings where the two **are** adjacent: glue them into a block, giving 7 objects in a circle =
`(7−1)! = 720`, times `2` for their internal order = `1440`.

`5040 − 1440 = **3600**`.

*Complementary counting on a circular arrangement. The `(n−1)!` is the part people miss —
rotations are identical, so one seat must be pinned before you count.*

</details>
